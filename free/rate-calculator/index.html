    How this freelance rate calculator works

    Most people pick a rate by looking at what others charge, then knocking a bit off to feel safe. That tells you nothing about whether the rate pays your bills. A floor rate starts with the life you want to fund, adds taxes and business costs, and spreads the total over the hours a client will actually pay for.

    Here is the exact formula the calculator uses:

    Pre-tax income = take-home income / (1 - tax rate) Costs = pre-tax income + (monthly expenses x 12) Revenue target = costs x (1 + profit margin) Billable hours = (52 - weeks off) x hours per week x utilization Minimum hourly rate = revenue target / billable hours

    The day rate is your hourly rate times one fifth of your weekly hours, so a 40 hour week gives an 8 hour day. The monthly target is the annual revenue divided by 12. It is what you need to invoice on average, not what lands in your account each month.

    The tax rate is one blended number: the share of gross income you expect to pay across income tax and any self employment or social charges. If you are not sure, ask an accountant for a rough figure.

    Why billable utilization is the number most freelancers get wrong

    Utilization is the share of your working hours that you can invoice. Many simple rate calculators skip it, which quietly assumes you bill every hour you work. Nobody does.

    Think about a normal week. You answer emails, write proposals, chase invoices, and take discovery calls that go nowhere. Then there are gaps between projects, when you are available but nobody is paying. All of that is real work. None of it is billable.

    Get this number wrong and the whole rate is wrong. With the example inputs above, moving utilization from 60% to 40% raises the floor from about $142 to about $214 an hour. Same life, same costs. The only change is being honest about how many hours you sell.

    Why you should quote above your floor

    The floor is the price at which the business just about works. It is not a target. Quote at the floor and every surprise comes out of your pocket: a project that runs long, a late payment, a quiet month.

    That is why the calculator suggests a quote about 20% above the floor. It gives you room to negotiate without dropping into the red. Many experienced freelancers go further: they price the outcome the client is buying, then check the fee against the floor. If a project cannot clear your floor, better to know before you say yes.

    A worked example

    Take the default inputs. This is an illustrative designer, not a benchmark:

    • Wants to keep $90,000 a year after tax, with a blended tax rate of 30%. That needs about $128,571 before tax.
    • Spends $1,200 a month on software, insurance, and equipment. That adds $14,400 a year.
    • Adds a 10% profit margin, which brings the revenue target to about $157,269.
    • Takes 6 weeks off and works 40 hours a week, so 46 working weeks and 1,840 working hours.
    • Bills 60% of those hours, which is 1,104 billable hours.

    $157,269 divided by 1,104 hours is about $142 an hour. The day rate is about $1,140, and the monthly revenue target is about $13,106. A sensible first quote would be $180 an hour or more.

    Notice how little of each invoice is take-home pay: about 57 cents of every dollar. The rest goes to taxes, costs, and the buffer that keeps the business alive.

    Frequently asked questions

    How much should a beginner freelancer charge?

    Start with your floor, not with what feels comfortable. Run this calculator with a lower utilization, around 30 to 40%, because new freelancers spend more time finding work. The result is often higher than beginners expect. If the market will not pay it yet, that is a signal to cut costs, niche down, or keep part time income while you build a client base. Avoid pricing far below your floor to win work. It is hard to raise rates on clients who came for a low price.

    Should I charge hourly or per project?

    Hourly is simple and fair when scope is unclear, such as ongoing support or open ended advice. Per project pricing usually works better when the deliverable is well defined, because you are rewarded for getting faster and the client gets a fixed number. Either way, know your floor first. A project fee should cover your estimated hours at your floor rate, plus a buffer for the unknowns.

    How do I raise my rates with existing clients?

    Give notice in writing, usually 30 to 60 days, and name the new rate and the date it starts. Keep the message short and do not apologize. Thank them for the work so far and offer to hold the current rate for a fixed block of work booked before the change. Raise rates for new clients first.

    How do I turn a salary into a freelance hourly rate?

    Do not just divide your salary by 2,080 hours. An employer pays for your holidays, your equipment, part of your taxes, and every hour you are not billable. Put the after tax pay you want into take-home income, add the benefits you used to get for free as monthly expenses, and set a realistic utilization. The calculator does the rest.

    What is a good billable utilization rate for a freelancer?

    For most solo freelancers, somewhere between 50 and 65% is a reasonable working assumption once sales, admin, and gaps are counted. New freelancers are often lower. People on a long retainer can be higher. The best number is your own, measured over a few months.

    Should my day rate be eight times my hourly rate?

    The calculator uses your weekly hours divided by five, so a 40 hour week gives an 8 hour day. If you discount full day bookings, start from your quoted rate, not your floor, so the discount never takes you below the minimum.

    What should I include in business expenses?

    Anything you pay to run the business: software and subscriptions, hardware spread over its useful life, insurance, accounting, a coworking desk, training, health cover, and retirement contributions an employer would have paid. Enter the monthly average. If a cost is yearly, divide it by 12.
