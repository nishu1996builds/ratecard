## R1. Lesson: the utilization trap

**Format:** lesson with worked numbers
**First comment:** Free calculator to run your own numbers: https://nishu1996builds.github.io/TBD-Claude/free/rate-calculator/

> Most freelance rate calculators assume you bill every hour you work. You won't.

Here's the math most people skip.

Take one example: you want $90,000 take-home, you pay 30% tax, you spend $1,200 a month running the business, you take 6 weeks off, work 40-hour weeks, and keep a 10% margin.

Your minimum hourly rate depends on how much of that time a client actually pays for:

- Bill 100% of hours: about $85/hr
- Bill 60%: about $142/hr
- Bill 40%: about $214/hr

Same life. Same costs. A 2.5x difference in the rate you need.

The gap is sales calls, proposals, invoicing, admin, and the weeks between projects. None of it is billable. All of it is work.

50% to 65% utilization is realistic for most solo businesses once you count that time honestly. If you set your rate assuming 100%, a slow quarter doesn't just hurt. It means you were underwater the whole time.

Price for the year you'll actually have, not the one on paper.

## R2. Mini-framework: three tiers, built from the middle out

**Format:** mini-framework
**First comment:** I built these checks into Ratecard's proposal builder: https://nishulegacy.gumroad.com/l/ratecard (code LAUNCH19 takes $5 off through Nov 20)

> A single price asks "should we hire you?" Three prices ask "which one do we want?"

That's the whole case for Good / Better / Best proposals. But most three-tier proposals are built wrong. People start with the cheapest option and add things.

Build from the middle out instead:

1. Write the package you actually want to sell. That's Better.
2. Strip it down for Good. It should solve the problem but leave obvious value on the table.
3. Load Best with speed, access, and ongoing support.

Then run four checks before you send it:

- Is Best at least 1.6x Better? If not, it doesn't make the middle look sensible.
- Is Better between 1.4x and 2.5x Good? Too close and the cheap option steals the sale. Too far and the jump feels steep.
- Are you visibly recommending the middle tier?
- Does each tier add something the client can see, not just more hours?

The top tier isn't there to be bought. It's there to make the middle feel reasonable. Now and then someone buys it anyway.

## R3. Myth vs reality: discounts

**Format:** myth vs reality
**First comment:** The full set of 10 tactics and scripts is inside Ratecard: https://nishulegacy.gumroad.com/l/ratecard

> Myth: a small discount helps you close the deal.

Reality: a discount tells the client your first number was padded. Now they'll push on every quote you send.

Myth: "That's too expensive" means the price is too high.

Reality: it means one of three things, and each needs a different answer. Ask which:

"Thanks for being direct. Is that compared to another quote, compared to the budget you had in mind, or compared to what you expect this to return?"

- Another quote: explain the difference in risk and outcome.
- Budget: offer the smaller tier, or split the work into phases.
- Return: rebuild the value math together, or part as friends.

Myth: the only way to meet a lower budget is to lower the price.

Reality: trade scope instead. Same rate, less work:

"I can work with that budget. To get there, we'd drop the usability testing and go to one revision round. Which of those matters less to you?"

The client gets a lower number. You keep your rate. Nobody learns that your prices are negotiable.

## R4. Checklist: before you send a proposal

**Format:** checklist
**First comment:** Ratecard does the floor rate, value math, and three-tier proposal in one offline file: https://nishulegacy.gumroad.com/l/ratecard (LAUNCH19 for $5 off, ends Nov 20)

> 8 checks before you hit send on a proposal.

Save this for the next one.

1. **Do you know your floor?** The number you can't go below after tax, expenses, and unbillable time. Never quote it. Quote at least 25% above it so you have room to trade.
2. **Did you restate the value first?** Put the client's problem in their own words and money before any price appears.
3. **What ROI does the client get at your price?** At 5x or better, price rarely becomes the argument. Under 3x, expect pushback or a request to phase the work.
4. **Are there three options?** One price is a yes/no question.
5. **Is the middle tier the one you recommend?**
6. **Is there a deposit?** 50% to start, the rest on milestones. Never all on completion.
7. **Are rush and change fees written in?** A fee agreed up front is a policy. A fee invented mid-project is an argument.
8. **Is there an expiry date?** "Valid until" stops a proposal from sitting open for months at an old price.

If you can't answer 1 and 3, the price is a guess.

## J1. Lesson: tailoring in 15 minutes, not 2 hours

**Format:** lesson / routine
**First comment:** Free checklist version of this routine: https://nishulegacy.gumroad.com/l/tailoring-checklist

> You don't need to rewrite your resume for every job. You need 15 minutes and a routine.

Most people either send the same resume everywhere or spend two hours rewriting it from scratch. Neither scales.

A routine that does:

**Minutes 0 to 3:** Read the posting twice. Highlight the skills and tools that appear more than once, and the outcomes they say they care about.

**Minutes 3 to 6:** Compare against your resume. Which highlighted terms are missing? Which ones do you have but describe in different words?

**Minutes 6 to 12:** Edit, don't rewrite. Use their wording where it's honestly true for you. Move your most relevant bullets to the top of each role. Rewrite your summary's first line for this job.

**Minutes 12 to 15:** Check the basics. File name, standard section headings, no tables or text boxes, and every claim is something you could explain in an interview.

The rule that keeps it honest: only use a keyword if you've done the thing. Tailoring is translation, not invention.

## J2. Mini-framework: the bullet that gets read

**Format:** mini-framework with before/after
**First comment:** The full Tailoring Playbook, 160 action verbs, and 3 ATS-safe templates are in The Job Search Kit: https://nishulegacy.gumroad.com/l/job-search-kit (code LAUNCH takes $10 off through Nov 19)

> "Responsible for" is the most expensive phrase on your resume.

It tells the reader what your job was. It doesn't tell them what happened because you did it.

A bullet that gets read has three parts:

**Action verb + what you did + what changed.**

Before:
"Responsible for managing the onboarding process for new clients."

After:
"Rebuilt client onboarding into a 5-step checklist, cutting setup time from two weeks to four days."

Before:
"Worked on social media."

After:
"Planned and scheduled a 3-month content calendar across two channels for a product launch."

Notice the second "after" has no big result. That's fine. Specific scope beats a vague result. If you don't have a number, describe the size of the thing: how many people, how many channels, how often, how long.

Three quick fixes:
- Delete "responsible for", "helped with", and "worked on"
- Start every bullet with a different verb
- Put the result in the first half of the bullet when you have one

(The examples above are illustrations, not real resumes.)

## J3. Myth vs reality: ATS

**Format:** myth vs reality
**First comment:** Free scanner that compares your resume to a job posting: https://nishu1996builds.github.io/tailorscan/

> Myth: "The ATS rejected me." Reality: the ATS probably couldn't read you.

Applicant tracking systems like Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, and iCIMS mostly store and sort applications. A person usually does the rejecting. But what that person sees depends on how well the system parsed your file.

**Myth:** A creative two-column layout stands out.
**Reality:** Columns, tables, and text boxes can scramble the parsed version. Your job titles end up next to the wrong dates.

**Myth:** You should hide keywords in white text.
**Reality:** Parsed text shows everything. A recruiter reading it will see exactly what you did.

**Myth:** Any section heading works.
**Reality:** Use standard ones: Experience, Education, Skills. Parsers look for them.

**Myth:** More keywords is better.
**Reality:** The right keywords, in context, in your bullets. A skills list stuffed with 40 tools reads as filler.

**Myth:** PDF always breaks ATS.
**Reality:** Follow what the posting asks for. If it doesn't say, a clean single-column Word file is the safest choice.

## J4. Checklist: run your job search like a sales pipeline

**Format:** checklist
**First comment:** The Job Search Kit includes a tracker (Excel and Google Sheets) with automatic follow-up dates and a funnel chart: https://nishulegacy.gumroad.com/l/job-search-kit

> I sell POS systems for a living. A job search is a sales pipeline with worse tracking.

In sales, nobody would send 80 pitches and then wait. You'd track every one and follow up on a schedule. Job seekers rarely do.

A simple pipeline checklist:

- **Log every application** the day you send it: company, role, date, link, contact name.
- **Set the follow-up date** at the same time. If you don't write it down, it won't happen.
- **Track stages, not just applications.** Applied, response, screen, interview, offer. The useful number is where people drop off.
- **Read the drop-off.** Lots of applications, no responses: the resume or targeting is the problem. Screens but no second rounds: the interview story is the problem. Fix the stage that leaks.
- **Find a person for every role you really want.** A referral or a direct note to the hiring manager beats a cold application.
- **Send the thank-you within a day.**
- **Review weekly.** Ten minutes on a Friday tells you what to change on Monday.

You can do this in any spreadsheet. I built one that fills in the follow-up dates and draws the funnel for you. Link in the first comment.
